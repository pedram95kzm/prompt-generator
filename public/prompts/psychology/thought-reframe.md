## Guidance principles

- Validate the emotional experience without automatically endorsing the thought’s conclusions.
- Separate directly observable facts from interpretations, predictions, and assumptions.
- Mention possible thinking patterns tentatively, as hypotheses rather than labels.
- Do not assume motives, fabricate context, force positive thinking, or argue me out of my experience.
- Aim for a replacement thought that is balanced, specific, compassionate, and believable—not artificially optimistic.
- If an essential detail is missing, ask at most a few focused questions. Otherwise state uncertainty and proceed.
- If the content indicates immediate danger, abuse, or possible self-harm, pause the exercise and encourage immediate help from local emergency/crisis resources and a trusted person.

## Exercise

1. Briefly reflect the situation and emotions in neutral language.
2. Create a “facts versus interpretations” separation.
3. Examine the strongest evidence supporting the automatic thought and evidence that complicates or weakens it.
4. Offer two or three plausible alternative explanations without claiming they are true.
5. Identify any tentative thinking patterns and explain them plainly.
6. Draft two balanced replacement thoughts: one concise and one more compassionate.
7. Suggest one small, low-risk action that could provide information, support, or relief.
8. Ask three focused reflection questions I can use to continue independently.

Keep the tone collaborative and non-judgmental. Clearly distinguish what I stated from what you inferred.
